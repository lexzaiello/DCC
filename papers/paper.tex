\section{Introduction}

Simply-typed combinatory logics are known to be equivalent to the simply-typed \(\lambda\)-calculus \autocite{Altenkirch2023CombinatoryLA}. These calculi can avoid contexts, and might enjoy benefits as such (e.g., unification). Work has been done on extending these logics with dependent types \autocite{altenkirch}, but no implementation has been proposed, nor have such theories been formalized.

\section{Background}

\subsection{The Untyped Categorical Combinators}

The categorical combinators \autocite{CURIEN1986188} are the basis of the Categorical Abstract Machine \autocite{cmachine}. As shown by Cousineau et al., they are a potential compilation target for the \(\lambda\)-calculus. Categorical combinators simulate variable substitution with projection over nested tuples (``couples'') constituting the ``environment'' and a curry combinator (\(\Lambda\)). The reduction rules for this calculus \eqref{red_untyped_cat} resemble the rules of the SK combinator calculus, and their categorical semantics are given by cartesian-closed categories \autocite[p. 178]{cmachine}.

\begin{figure}[h!]\label{red_untyped_cat}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l l}
  (ass) & \((x \circ y) z = x(y z)\) \\
  (fst) & \(Fst(a, b) = a\) \\
  (snd) & \(Snd(a, b) = b\) \\
  (dpair) & \(\langle x, y\rangle z = (x z, y z)\) \\
  (curr) & \(\Lambda(x)yz = x(y, z)\) \\
  (app) & \(App(f, x) = f x\) \\
  (quote) & \(`(x) y = x\)
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\caption{Reduction rules of the untyped combinators as seen in the Categorical Abstract Machine.}
\end{figure}

\section{Dependent Categorical Combinators}

Our primary insights when extending the categorical combinators to dependent types are: when typed as \(\Sigma\)-pairs, couples can naturally encode contexts and typing judgments; pairs which produce couples correspond to substitution within contexts; the types of all primimtive objects can be represented without the pair rule (the affine restriction) after adding a \(Flip\) combinator. By showing that the affine restriction is strongly normalizing, we gain decidable judgmental equality (\(\equiv\)) and a decision procedure for type-checking.

As usual, we extend the calculus with a \(\Pi(m, n)\) object. Arrows \(\alpha \rightarrow \beta\) are represented by \(\Pi\) objects that quote their codomain. We re-interpret the base combinators as pertaining to \(\Sigma\) couples, instead of product couples.

\subsection{Couples as Dependent Sums}

We interpret couples \((a, b)\) as sigma terms like such:

\begin{center}\label{coup_exp}
\begin{figure}[h!]\label{coup_dep}
\begin{center}
\begin{equation}
\inferrule[Coup]
{ \Gamma \vdash \alpha : \sort{m}, \beta : \alpha \rightarrow \sort{n}, x : \alpha, y : \beta x}
{\Gamma \vdash \llbracket \beta \rrbracket (a, b) : (\Sigma(m, n) \alpha \beta)}
\end{equation}
\end{center}
\caption{Couples as Dependent Sums}
\end{figure}
\end{center}

We require that \(\beta : \alpha \rightarrow \sort{m}\) be given explicitly in couples through the type annotation \(\llbracket \beta \rrbracket\). Similarly, we add type arguments to pairs (\(\llbracket m, \alpha, \beta\rrbracket \langle f, g\rangle\)) which allow the resulting couple's \texttt{snd} component to depend on the type of the argument \(x\), the term \(x\), and \(f x\).

This view of couples as sigma terms naturally encodes the notion of judgments. For example, the term \(\llbracket Id(\sort{m})\rrbracket (\alpha, x)\) corresponds to the judgment \(x : \alpha\). Similarly, a context \(\Gamma\) can be encoded as a nested couple:

\begin{equation}
  x_0 : A_0, x_1 : A_1(x_0), x_2 : A_2 (x_0, x_1) \approx
  \llbracket A_2 \rrbracket (\llbracket \llbracket A_1 \rrbracket (x_0, x_1), x_2)
\end{equation}

In the categorical abstract machine, this encoding of judgments corresponds to an environment where variable assignments are explicitly typed. We use this encoding in our inference ruls to avoid copying types under \(\Pi\) binders.

\subsection{Type Annotations as Points via Couple-Judgments}

Except for \(\Pi\) and \(\Sigma\), all combinator objects have type annotations specified as points. For example, the projection \(Fst(\alpha, \beta)\) is indexed by terms dnoting the type of the expected \(\Sigma\)-couple. This contradicts intuition that type arguments must be able to depend on other terms, and thus, cannot be points. However, by referring to types in the context as the \text{snd} component of a \(\Sigma\)-couple (\(\Sigma(m, n)\ \sort{m}\ Id(\sort{m})\)), they only need to be mentioned by their respective sorts. This convention is modeled after the categorical combinators, where curry (\(\Lambda(x)\)), pairs, and couples mention their arguments as points. As a result, we avoid another potential duplication of type-points inside the combinators' types.

See \eqref{point_free_derivation} where we derive point-free \(Id\ \alpha\) using the dependent curry (\(\Lambda\)) combinator and \(\Sigma\)-couples.

\subsection{Inference Rules}

We make use of an infinite sort hierarchy to avoid paradoxes (i.e., \(\text{Sort} : \text{Sort}\)).

\subsubsection{Application and \(\Pi\)}

\begin{equation}
  \inferrule[App]
  { \Gamma \vdash f : \Pi(m, n)\ \alpha\ \beta, x : \alpha}
  { \Gamma \vdash f x : \beta\ \llbracket Id(\sort{m}) \rrbracket (\alpha, x)}
\end{equation}

The inference rule for function application is as usual, except the codomain \(\beta\) is indexed by the judgment \(x : \alpha\) encoded as a \(\Sigma\)-couple. This convention is indispensible in dependent types. For example, in \(\forall (\alpha : \sort{m}) (x : \alpha), \beta\), \(\alpha\) may be mentioned in \(\beta\) even though it has been consumed by \(x : \alpha\). By supplying \(\beta\) with \(x\) and its type, \(\alpha\) never needs to be copied, as long as it appears at most once under each subsequent \(\Pi\) binder.\\

We define nondependent arrows (\(\alpha \rightarrow \beta\)) as such:

\begin{equation}
  ((\alpha : \sort{m}) \rightarrow (\beta : \sort{n})) = \Pi(m, n)\ \alpha\ `(\sort{n}, \Sigma(\sort{n}, Id(\sort{m})), \beta)
\end{equation}

Since we do not make use of variables, we encode \(\Pi\)-types as application of a \(\Pi(m, n)\) combinator (where \(m\) and \(n\) are universe levels). Using our definition of arrows and our application inference rule, we give the inference rule for \(\Pi(m, n)\):

\begin{equation}
  \inferrule
  { }
  { \Gamma \vdash \Pi(m, n) : \sort{m} \rightarrow ((\Sigma(\sort{m}, Id(\sort{m})) \rightarrow \sort{n}) \rightarrow \sort{n}) }
\end{equation}

Our judgment-couple convention allows the type of \(\Pi\) to be nondependent. This proves to be useful in several other combinator types, avoiding duplication of elements in the context.

\subsubsection{Couple Projections}

The inference rules for projection on \(\Sigma\)-couples are as such:\\

\begin{tabular}{c}
  \inferrule[Fst]
  {\Gamma \vdash \alpha : \sort{m}, \beta : \alpha \rightarrow \sort{n}}
  {\Gamma \vdash Fst(\alpha, \beta) : \Sigma(\alpha, \beta) \rightarrow \alpha}\\\\
  \inferrule[Snd]
  {\Gamma \vdash \alpha : \sort{m}, \beta : \alpha \rightarrow \sort{n}}
  {\Gamma \vdash Snd(\alpha, \beta) : \Pi(\text{max m n \(m\) \(n\)}, n)\ \Sigma(\alpha, \beta)\ (\beta \circ Fst(\alpha, \beta) \circ Snd'(\sort{\text{max \(m\) \(n\)}}, \Sigma(\alpha, \beta)))}\\\\
  \inferrule[Snd']
  {\Gamma \vdash \alpha : \sort{m}, \beta : \alpha \rightarrow \sort{n}}
  {\Gamma \vdash Snd'(\alpha, \beta) : \Sigma(\alpha, \beta) \rightarrow \beta}\\\\
\end{tabular}

The nondependent \(Snd'(\alpha, \beta)\) can be replaced in these types by \(Snd(\sort{m}, Id(\sort{m}))\). However, \(g\) in \(f \circ g\) is not dependent (so as not to duplicate the argument \(x\)), and as such, \(Snd'(\alpha, \beta)\) must be used instead.

\subsection{Derivation: Point-Free \(Id\)}

\begin{lemma}\label{point_free_derivation}
  There exists \(Id_{m} : \forall (\alpha : \sort{m}), \alpha \rightarrow \alpha\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  Recall the types of \(Id\), \(\Lambda(\beta, x)\), and \(Snd(\alpha, \beta)\):\\\\
  \begin{tabular}{c}
  \inferrule[Id]
    {}
    {\Gamma \vdash Id(\alpha) : \alpha \rightarrow \alpha}\\\\
  \inferrule[Curry]
    {\Gamma \vdash x : \Pi(\text{max \(m\) \(n\)}, o)\ \Sigma(\alpha, \beta)\ y, \beta : \alpha \rightarrow \sort{n}}
    {\Gamma \vdash \Lambda(\beta, x) : \Pi(m, o)\ \alpha\ ((Flip(\Pi(n, o))\ \gamma)\ \circ\ (\beta\ \circ\ Snd'(\sort{m}, \alpha)))}\\\\
  \inferrule[Snd]
    {}
    {\Gamma \vdash Snd(\alpha, \beta) : \Pi(\text{max \(m\) \(n\)}, n)\ \Sigma(\alpha, \beta)\ (\beta \circ Fst(\alpha, \beta) \circ Snd'(\sort{\text{max \(m\) \(n\)}}, \Sigma(\alpha, \beta)))}\\\\
  \end{tabular}\\\\
  So, let \(Id_{m}\) be as follows:
  \begin{align*}
    Id_{m} := \Lambda(Id(\sort{m}), &\\
    \Lambda(Snd(\sort{m}, Id(\sort{m})), &\\
    & Snd(\Sigma(\sort{m}, Id(\sort{m})), Snd(\sort{m}, Id(\sort{m}))))) \sort{m}
  \end{align*}.
\end{proof}

Our reduction rules are otherwise the same as in the categorical abstract machine \autocite{cmachine}. We use \(f(x_1, \ldots, x_n)\) to denote a combinator requiring \(n\) type annotations. Type annotations for pairs and couples are given by \(\llbracket \ldots \rrbracket\). Application is denoted by juxtaposition:

\begin{center}
\begin{figure}[h!]\label{red_untyped_cat}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l l}
  (ass) & \((x \circ y) z \rightarrow x(y z)\) \\
  (fst) & \(Fst(\alpha, \beta)\ \llbracket \beta \rrbracket (a, b) \rightarrow a\) \\
  (snd) & \(Snd(\alpha, \beta)\ \llbracket \beta \rrbracket (a, b) \rightarrow b\) \\
  (curr) & \(\Lambda(\beta, x)yz \rightarrow x\ \llbracket \beta \rrbracket(y, z)\) \\
  (app) & \(App(\gamma, \beta)\ \llbracket \beta \rrbracket (f, x) \rightarrow f x\) \\
  (quote) & \(\text{`}(\alpha, \beta, x) y \rightarrow x\) \\
  (flip) & \(Flip(x)y z \rightarrow x z y\)\\
  (dpair) & \(\llbracket m, \alpha, \beta \rrbracket \langle x, y\rangle\ z \rightarrow \llbracket \gamma \llbracket Id(\sort{m}) \rrbracket (\alpha, x) \rrbracket (x z, y z)\)
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\caption{Reduction rules of the dependently-typed categorical combinators}
\end{figure}
\end{center}
